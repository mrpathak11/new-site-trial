Nepal’s Earthquake Preparedness: Resilience, Risk Monitoring, and Comprehensive Strategies

A decade after the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, Nepal has advanced in preparedness and resilience, but challenges persist in a nation sitting atop active tectonic stress.

Suman PathakMechanical & Energy Systems Engineer, Freiburg

Contents

    Contents

      Nepal, nestled in the seismically active Himalayan belt, contends with a spectrum of natural hazards — earthquakes, landslides, glacial lake outburst floods (GLOFs), and avalanches. The devastating Gorkha earthquake of April 25, 2015, with a magnitude of 7.8, claimed nearly 9,000 lives, displaced millions, and exposed the nation’s vulnerability.

      A decade later, as of April 2025, Nepal has advanced meaningfully in preparedness, resilience, and risk monitoring. But challenges persist. Recent seismic events underscore the urgency of continued effort. This article examines Nepal’s progress, its remaining weaknesses, the unique hazards of the Himalayan context, and the role of community resilience and international collaboration.

      Recent Events: April 2025 Update

      On April 4, 2025, a 5.0-magnitude earthquake struck western Nepal, centred in Jajarkot, with tremors felt across northern India including Patna and Uttar Pradesh. The quake occurred at a depth of 20 km and caused no reported casualties or significant structural damage, per Nepal’s National Earthquake Monitoring and Research Center (NEMRC) and India’s National Centre for Seismology (NCS).

      However, it rattled communities still recovering from the 2023 Jajarkot quake, which killed 153 people. Local authorities deployed response teams, and the Nepal Red Cross activated recovery protocols using pre-positioned supplies. This event follows a pattern of frequent tremors — 38 quakes above magnitude 2 in Nepal over the preceding 30 days, including a 5.1-magnitude event near Lobuche on March 8, 2025.

      Seismologists warn that western Nepal remains at high risk for a major quake potentially exceeding magnitude 6, due to unrelieved tectonic stress accumulated since 2015. The April 2025 event prompted renewed calls for stricter building code enforcement and community preparedness drills.

      Progress in Earthquake Preparedness and Resilience

      Since the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, Nepal has prioritised reconstruction and resilience-building. The National Reconstruction Authority (NRA), formed in December 2015, has rebuilt 74% of 7,553 schools with seismic-resistant designs by 2021, with 20% still in progress as of 2025. Housing reconstruction has reached approximately 90% completion, emphasising safer structural standards.

      The 2017 Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act aligns Nepal’s policy framework with the Sendai Framework (2015–2030), embedding resilience — defined as the capacity to anticipate, withstand, adapt, and recover — into national legislation and planning.

      Community Resilience and International Collaboration

      The Nepal Risk Reduction Consortium (NRRC), launched in 2011, fosters collaborative disaster risk reduction across government, civil society, and international partners. Community-level efforts have been essential: the Program for Enhancement of Emergency Response (PEER), backed by USAID, has trained over 737 first responders from the Nepal Army, Police, and Red Cross.

      Grassroots resilience is visible. Educational programmes in 22 western Nepal schools, annual Earthquake Safety Day drills, and the retrofitting of hospitals and schools have strengthened community-level preparedness. Twelve warehouses managed by the Nepal Red Cross — stocked with USAID-funded emergency supplies — enable rapid response, as seen following the April 2025 Jajarkot event.

      Remaining Challenges

      Despite these advances, significant gaps remain. Building code enforcement in informal urban settlements is inconsistent. Kathmandu, home to millions of people in densely built informal structures, remains one of the most earthquake-vulnerable cities in Asia. Open space preservation and landslide monitoring — particularly critical in the context of climate-driven precipitation changes — require sustained investment and coordination.

      Rural communities in remote mountain districts still face significant response time delays due to infrastructure limitations. International humanitarian logistics, while improved, remain constrained by Nepal’s challenging terrain.

      Conclusion

      Nepal’s earthquake preparedness story is one of genuine progress paired with persistent vulnerability. The April 2025 Jajarkot quake, though less destructive than the 2015 event, is a reminder that seismic risk remains high and vigilance cannot be relaxed.

      Resilience — built through policy, community action, retrofitted infrastructure, and sustained international collaboration — offers Nepal a path toward managing this risk more effectively. A unified, data-driven, and community-centric approach, attuned to both seismic and climate-related hazards, is the most credible way forward.
